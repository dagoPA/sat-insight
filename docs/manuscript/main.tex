\title{\textbf{How much neighbourhood detail do published statistics contain?\\
An exchange rate between aggregate social data and satellite maps}}
\author[1]{Dagoberto Pulido Arias}
\affil[1]{Independent researcher}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent Most countries publish social statistics as administrative aggregates and
almost none publish them at neighbourhood level. We measure how much neighbourhood
detail those aggregates contain. Training a weakly supervised model on nothing but
five-level deprivation grades of Mexican municipalities and free satellite imagery, and
validating against fully held-out census-tract grades, we find that municipal aggregates
recover 80\% of the tract-level ordering attainable with full spatial supervision
(within-municipality $\rho$ = 0.213 of a 0.266 ceiling). One hundred aggregates already
recover most of it; coarsening the label to state level halves it, and to one national
figure erases it. The resulting maps close 26--41\% of the gap between aggregate-guided
and census-guided targeting of social programmes, replicate against an independent
institution's index ($\rho$ = 0.37), outperform the leading downloadable wealth map, and
transfer without retraining to Bogotá ($\rho$ = 0.38 against block-level strata). Popular
attention-based multiple-instance models fail at this task for a measurable reason we
diagnose and fix.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Introduction}

Census-quality social data is expensive, and most of the world sees it roughly once a
decade. In between, and below the level the tables are published at, policy runs on
aggregates: a municipal index here, a district average there. A rich line of work has
shown that satellite imagery plus machine learning can predict such indicators across
space~\cite{jean2016,yeh2020,chi2022,burke2021}, and global products derived from that
idea are already used for programme targeting~\cite{chi2022,aiken2022}. But prediction
across places and \emph{disaggregation within them} are different tasks, and the second
one carries the policy weight: a mayor knows their municipality is poor; what they lack
is the map of which neighbourhoods are poorest.

This paper asks the disaggregation question quantitatively: \emph{how much neighbourhood
detail does a published aggregate contain?} We treat the answer as an exchange rate ---
so many aggregates of such a size buy so much map --- and we measure it where it can be
measured: Mexico publishes its Social Deprivation Grade (GRS) both as a municipal
aggregate and at census-tract level (AGEB) for 61{,}430 urban
tracts~\cite{coneval2020}. We train only on the former and validate only on the latter,
held out in full, on cities the model never saw.

Three design choices make the measurement clean. First, supervision is genuinely weak: a
model sees a bag of image tokens for a municipality and one label --- the shares of
population at each deprivation grade --- and never any spatial annotation. Second, every
comparison is scored \emph{within} municipalities, because ordering tracts across cities
is mostly solved by knowing which city is poor, and pooled metrics flatter every method
(pooled $\rho$ = 0.44 against 0.21 within). Third, an oracle trained on the held-out
tract labels bounds what the frozen representation supports at all; every result is
reported as a fraction of that ceiling, so ``how good is weak supervision'' is separated
from ``how good are the features''.

\section*{Results}

\subsection*{Attention does not localize, and the reason is measurable}

We first trained the standard tool for weakly supervised localization: gated-attention
multiple-instance learning (ABMIL~\cite{ilse2018}, with the CLAM instance
constraint~\cite{lu2021}). It predicts municipal labels well (quadratic-weighted
$\kappa$ = 0.62) and its attention map is at chance (AUROC 0.463 for recovering
high-deprivation tracts; Fig.~\ref{fig:dissociation}a). The diagnosis is quantitative:
flattening the attention to near-uniform via an entropy penalty costs no bag accuracy
(AUROC 0.899 $\to$ 0.895), so the bag task is solvable from the plain feature mean and
no gradient ever asks the attention to point anywhere. Four ablations (ordinal and
cumulative labels, clustering constraints, entropy sweeps) confirm the pattern.

Inverting the composition fixes it. Predicting every token first and averaging the
\emph{predictions} --- learning from label proportions (LLP~\cite{quadrianto2009}) ---
makes the map the mechanism rather than a by-product: the same features, bags and labels
reach AUROC 0.761. Because Mexican municipal grades are literally population-weighted
aggregates of tract grades, the setting is LLP with known instance weights, and using
census population as those weights improves the map further (within-municipality $\rho$
0.213 $\to$ 0.234, bag error 0.090 $\to$ 0.087). A hyperparameter sweep --- chosen on
training folds only, under the one signal weak supervision may legitimately monitor ---
accounts for most of the remaining gap to the ceiling
(Fig.~\ref{fig:dissociation}a).

The sweep exposed a structural blindness worth naming. Across fourteen configurations,
the rank correlation between bag-level error (the honest model-selection criterion) and
map quality is $-0.14$: selecting weakly supervised models by the only metric they may
look at is nearly blind to the quantity that matters
(Fig.~\ref{fig:dissociation}b). This applies to any pipeline whose product is the map
--- computational pathology included --- and it is why we release our benchmark with
held-out instance labels.

\subsection*{The exchange rate: three axes}

Figure~\ref{fig:curve} shows the headline measurement on an expanded pool of 1{,}144
municipal bags covering essentially all of urban Mexico. \textbf{Quantity}
(Fig.~\ref{fig:curve}a): fifty aggregates buy $\rho$ = 0.15, one hundred buy 0.18, and
the full national supply buys 0.213 --- 80\% of the 0.266 oracle ceiling. The curve's
knee is early: most of the attainable map exists with a few hundred published numbers.
\textbf{Granularity} (Fig.~\ref{fig:curve}b): re-labelling the same bags with state-level
aggregates halves the map ($\rho$ = 0.119); a single national aggregate erases it
(0.004). What a statistics office chooses to publish is, quite literally, the map its
users can recover. \textbf{Resolution} (Fig.~\ref{fig:curve}c): destroying the optical
resolution to the radar's 20 m by block-averaging barely hurts ($\rho$ = 0.183 $\to$
0.172), while radar itself collapses to chance (0.006) despite being the best single
modality for \emph{predicting} municipal grades. Content, not ground sampling distance,
is what fails: the dissociation between predicting and localizing recurs across sensors,
not just across models.

\subsection*{External validity}

Four independent checks argue the map is measurement rather than artifact
(Fig.~\ref{fig:validation}). It outperforms the leading downloadable product --- Meta's
Relative Wealth Index~\cite{chi2022} --- under our exact protocol ($\rho$ = 0.107
$\pm$ 0.082 vs 0.213; Fig.~\ref{fig:validation}a), and a land-cover-plus-NDVI head with
no learned representation reaches 0.149, so the foundation-model features carry real
signal beyond built-up density. It replicates against an institution it never trained
toward: CONAPO's continuous urban marginalization index, built from a different
indicator set, correlates at $\rho$ = 0.366 $\pm$ 0.073 within municipalities ---
higher than against the five-level training construct, as a tie-free target should be
(Fig.~\ref{fig:validation}b). In simulated geographic targeting, allocating a fixed
budget by the map instead of by the municipal aggregate closes 26--41\% of the gap to
census-guided allocation, growing with budget (Fig.~\ref{fig:validation}c). And a
border-discontinuity control finds no administrative shortcut: crossing a municipal
border where deprivation does not change moves the map by $+0.013$
[$-0.006, +0.247$] once neighbourhood context is computed city-wide --- an earlier
$+0.143$ jump traced to bag-limited adjacency, our seam rather than the model's ---
while tract borders inside municipalities are crossed smoothly ($-0.002$).

\subsection*{The construct travels; a different construct does not}

Applied unchanged to Bogotá, the Mexican model orders Colombia's block-level
socioeconomic strata at $\rho$ = 0.378 (AUROC 0.732 for strata 1--2;
Fig.~\ref{fig:transfer}). Applied to Rio de Janeiro, it detects IBGE's mapped informal
settlements barely above chance (AUROC 0.581 among built-up tokens). The contrast is
informative: Colombian strata measure the same construct as the GRS --- a graded
deprivation ordering over the whole urban fabric --- while the AGSN mask delimits
informality, a partly orthogonal notion. Weak supervision on aggregates taught the model
a deprivation gradient, and that gradient, not a country signature, is what transfers.

\subsection*{Limits}

The ceiling itself is the honest limit: even with full tract labels and neighbourhood
context, 160 m tokens support $\rho$ = 0.266 within municipalities. Agreement rises
steeply with tract size (from 0.18 for tracts of 1--3 tokens to 0.45 for 20--49;
Supplementary Table) --- the modifiable areal unit problem stated in units an office can
act on. Maps at this fidelity order neighbourhoods usefully for prioritization and do
not read individual blocks.

\section*{Discussion}

The exchange rate reframes a familiar technology. Rather than another demonstration that
imagery predicts poverty, it answers the question a statistics office would actually
ask: \emph{what resolution of publication buys what resolution of inference?} The three
axes give the answer an operational shape --- a few hundred aggregates suffice; publish
municipal rather than state tables; optical resolution is not the binding constraint ---
and the targeting experiment prices it in people reached. The negative results carry
equal weight: attention-based MIL fails here for a structural reason, aggregate-level
validation is nearly blind to map quality, and informality is not a free by-product of a
deprivation gradient. All three are warnings any weakly supervised mapping effort will
meet.

Mexico's dual-level publication made the calibration possible; the measured fractions
are what travel to the majority of countries that publish only aggregates. The Bogotá
result is the first step of that argument, and extending the dual calibration to
Colombia's own strata is the natural next measurement.

\section*{Methods}

\noindent\textbf{Data.} Sentinel-2 L2A and Sentinel-1 RTC annual median composites for
2020 over 424 Mexican cities and municipalities (10 m grid, windowed reads from
Planetary Computer), CONEVAL GRS at municipal and AGEB level (2020 census), CONAPO IMU
2020, ESA WorldCover 2020, Meta RWI, IBGE AGSN 2019, Bogotá block stratification.
\textbf{Bags and instances.} A bag is a municipality; instances are 16-pixel tokens
(160 m) encoded by a frozen DOFA foundation model~\cite{xiong2024}; both sensors are
fused per token. Labels are the population shares at each grade threshold.
\textbf{Model.} A two-layer head over frozen features predicts four cumulative-grade
probabilities per token; the bag prediction is the (population-weighted) mean of token
predictions, fit with binary cross-entropy on standardized inputs; each token's hidden
representation is concatenated with the mean of its 8 grid neighbours (480 m).
\textbf{Protocol.} 110 training / 14 validation / 14 test cities, stratified, no city
split across sets; the test set remains unopened. All sweeps select on grouped training
folds; validation cities are scored once per reported configuration; comparisons are
paired by municipality over three seeds; intervals are city-clustered bootstraps.
\textbf{Oracle.} The same head trained directly on tract labels bounds the
representation. \textbf{Code and data.} Full pipeline, frozen vectors, labels, splits
and protocol released as a benchmark.
